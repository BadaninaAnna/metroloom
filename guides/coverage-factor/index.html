Coverage factor k and the Welch–Satterthwaite formula

By Anna Badanina, MSc in Metrology and Information-Measuring Technology · 8 November 2026

The expanded uncertainty is U = k · uc. Almost every certificate says k = 2, and in most calibrations that is correct. But k = 2 gives a coverage probability of about 95 % only if the combined uncertainty is known well, that is, if it has many degrees of freedom. When a Type A component with few readings dominates the budget, k = 2 understates U.

Why the number of readings matters

A standard deviation calculated from 4 readings is itself an uncertain estimate. To keep the same coverage probability, the interval must be wider. Statistically, the coverage factor then comes from Student's t-distribution instead of the normal distribution, with the degrees of freedom ν of the estimate.

The Welch–Satterthwaite formula

A budget combines several components, each with its own degrees of freedom νi (n − 1 for Type A, usually ∞ for Type B). The effective degrees of freedom of the combined uncertainty are approximated by:

νeff = uc⁴ / Σ ( (ci · u(xi))⁴ / νi )

Components with νi = ∞ add nothing to the sum. The fourth powers mean that only the large components with few degrees of freedom matter. If νeff is not an integer, EA-4/02 says to round it down to the next lower integer. Then k is the t-quantile for that ν and the chosen coverage probability.

Worked example: micrometer with four readings

A 10 mm pin is measured four times with a digital micrometer: 10.012, 10.018, 10.009 and 10.015 mm.

νeff = 0.00280⁴ / (0.00194⁴ / 3) = 13.09 → 13

For ν = 13 and 95.45 %, k = 2.21. So U = 2.21 × 0.00280 = 0.0062 mm. With k = 2 out of habit, U would have been 0.0056 mm, about 10 % too small. Repeatability makes up almost half of the variance here, and three degrees of freedom are not many.

Two ways to bring k back towards 2: take more readings, or use a pooled standard deviation from an earlier repeatability study with many degrees of freedom (see Type A and Type B uncertainty).

A note on EA-4/02 table E.1. While validating Metroloom we found that the table prints k = 2.24 for ν = 11. The exact t-quantile is 2.2549, which rounds to 2.25 and fits between 2.28 (ν = 10) and 2.23 (ν = 12). Metroloom uses the exact value. Details in the validation report.

95 % or 95.45 %?

EA-4/02 and most accredited calibration certificates use 95.45 %, the probability that corresponds exactly to k = 2 for a normal distribution. Some fields use 95 %, which gives k = 1.96 for ν = ∞. The difference is small, but state on the certificate which one you used, together with k.

When Welch–Satterthwaite is not enough

The formula assumes uncorrelated inputs and a combined distribution close to normal. If one rectangular component dominates the budget, the combined distribution is closer to rectangular than normal, and the t-based k can be too large. For such cases, and for strongly non-linear models, the Monte Carlo method of GUM Supplement 1 (JCGM 101:2008) gives a more reliable interval.
